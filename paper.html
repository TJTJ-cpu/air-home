Single-room ventilation trial · Observational

The Gap Beats the Door

A fan exhausting through a narrow gap ventilated an occupied bedroom better than a wide-open sliding door — and let in half as much particulate matter while doing it.

23–26 August 2026 3 nights, 3 configurations 2 occupants, sleeping 780 observations at 3-minute intervals
Abstract

Carbon dioxide accumulates overnight in occupied bedrooms because sleeping adults exhale it faster than an unventilated room can dilute it. We measured CO2, temperature, humidity and particulates continuously across three consecutive nights in the same room with the same two occupants, changing only the ventilation path: all doors closed; one sliding door fully open; and a door left ajar with a fan exhausting room air outward.

Fitting a single-zone mass-balance model with a shared occupant source term gives air exchange rates of 0.10 h−1 sealed, 0.27 h−1 with the door open, and 0.33 h−1 with the fan — a 2.7× and 3.3× improvement respectively. Peak CO2 fell from 1496 to 1064 to 979 ppm. The fan configuration is the first whose fitted equilibrium (934 ppm) sits below the 1000 ppm guideline, meaning it would stay under that threshold however long the night ran; it never crossed it once.

Particulates did not follow the same order. The fully open door more than doubled mean PM2.5 relative to the sealed night; the fan through a narrow gap raised it far less. On both CO2 and particulates, the smaller opening with active extraction beat the larger passive one.

With one night per configuration these results cannot be separated from ordinary night-to-night variation. They are reported as three well-characterised observations, not a controlled result.

01 The three configurations

Night 1 · 23 Aug

Sealed

Every door closed. No fan. The control condition, and the one most bedrooms default to overnight.

Night 2 · 24 Aug

Sliding door open

One sliding door left fully open. Passive ventilation only — air moves by temperature difference and whatever breeze exists.

Night 3 · 25 Aug

Gap plus exhaust fan

Door open only slightly, with a fan inside the room blowing air out through the gap. Active extraction through a small opening.

Occupancy, bedding, clothing and the room itself were unchanged across all three nights. Two adults slept in the room each night. Only the ventilation path differed.

02 Method

2.1  Instrumentation

Readings came from a consumer air quality monitor (INKBIRD PLUS) reporting CO2, temperature, relative humidity, AQI, PM2.5 and PM10. The device has a display but no data output of any kind — no app, no logging, no port.

To capture it, a webcam photographed the display every three minutes and a vision-language model (Qwen3-VL-8B, running locally) transcribed the digits from each photograph into a database. Each reading is timestamped from the host computer's clock at the moment of capture, not from the clock shown on the device, which was found to drift. Values outside physically plausible bounds were rejected as misreads rather than stored.

2.2  Defining a night

A night runs 20:00 to 09:00 local time, deliberately wider than sleep itself. The hour before bed establishes the baseline the climb is measured from, and the hour after waking captures how quickly the room clears. Analysis of the build-up begins at the CO2 minimum preceding the peak, which marks the room closing up.

2.3  Model, and why the source term is shared

Treating the room as a single well-mixed zone with a constant source and constant exchange with outdoor air gives a first-order mass balance whose solution is:

C(t) = Cout + (S/λ)(1 − e−λt) + (C0 − Cout)e−λt λ — air changes per hour (h−1)  ·  S — source strength, occupant CO2 per room volume (ppm h−1)  ·  C0 — concentration at occupancy  ·  Cout — outdoor baseline, 401 ppm from the 1st percentile of all readings

Fitting λ and S freely for each night separately produces an incoherent result: the fan night comes out with a source strength of 99 ppm/h against the sealed night's 189, as though the occupants had exhaled half as much CO2. They had not. The two parameters trade off against one another, and a build-up that never reaches equilibrium does not constrain them independently.

Since the same two people slept in the same room every night, S must be constant. We therefore fit all three nights jointly: one shared S, one λ per night, minimising total squared residuals. This is both more defensible and more informative, and it is the fit reported throughout. The best-fit shared source strength is 176.5 ppm/h.

04 Discussion

A small opening with a fan beat a large opening without one. This is the headline finding and it is not obvious in advance. The sliding door presented a far larger aperture than a door left ajar, yet the fan configuration achieved a 23% higher air exchange rate. Passive ventilation depends on whatever pressure difference the weather happens to supply; a fan manufactures one. On a still night the large opening has little to drive flow through it, while the fan works regardless.

The sealed room was nowhere near finished filling. Its measured peak was 1496 ppm, but the joint fit projects equilibrium at 2178 ppm. The occupants woke with the room roughly two thirds of the way to its steady state. A longer night, or a later alarm, would have meant materially worse air — the measured peak substantially understates how poor that configuration is.

Ventilation and particulates do not rank the same way. Mean PM2.5 was lowest sealed (2.70 µg/m³), highest with the door fully open (6.16), and intermediate with the fan (3.77). The open door is a large uncontrolled aperture that lets outdoor particulates in freely. The fan draws makeup air through a much smaller gap, so less gets in per unit of air exchanged. That the fan wins on both axes — more air changes, fewer particulates — is the practical result worth keeping. All three nights stayed well below the WHO 24-hour guideline of 15 µg/m³, so the trade was not costly here; near a road, or during cooking or burning season, it could be.

Absolute ventilation is now adequate, just. Converting the fitted rates gives roughly 2.1 L/s of fresh air per person sealed, 5.6 with the door open and 6.9 with the fan. Common design guidance for bedrooms sits around 7–10 L/s per person. The fan configuration reaches the bottom of that band; the other two do not come close.

Model fit is worst for the fan night (R² 0.894 against 0.959 and 0.962). This is expected and mildly informative. A fan does not produce the smooth, constant exchange the model assumes — flow depends on the gap, on door position, and on whether anything obstructed it during the night. The residual structure is a hint that the fan night is less well described by a single constant λ than the passive nights are.

05 Limitations

  • One night per configuration. This remains the binding limitation. With n = 1 there is no way to separate the effect of a configuration from ordinary night-to-night variation, because that variation has still never been measured. Repeating any one condition twice would establish it, and nothing here should be treated as a settled effect size until that is done.
  • Not randomised, and confounded with time. The three conditions ran on three consecutive nights in increasing order of ventilation. Any trend in weather, outdoor CO2 or household routine across those days is indistinguishable from the intervention — and a monotone trend would produce exactly the monotone result observed. Alternating the order would break this.
  • The nights did not start identically. Occupancy began at 20:00, 20:43 and 20:20, at 439, 547 and 557 ppm respectively. The model accounts for differing starting concentration but not for the differing occupied duration.
  • Readings are not statistically independent. Lag-1 autocorrelation is 0.998 — consecutive readings three minutes apart are nearly identical. The effective sample size is closer to three nights than to 780 observations, which is why no p-values appear anywhere in this document. A significance test treating these as independent samples would be badly wrong.
  • The single-zone assumption is imperfect. Inverting the shared source strength for two sleeping adults implies a zone volume near 150 m³, larger than a typical bedroom. The room is evidently not a sealed zone: it exchanges air with the rest of the dwelling, so λ describes a room-plus-adjacent-space zone rather than the bedroom alone. Absolute L/s figures inherit that uncertainty; the ratios between nights are more trustworthy than the absolute values.
  • Uncalibrated sensor, transcribed by a model. The monitor was never checked against a reference instrument, so absolute accuracy is unknown. Digits were transcribed by a vision model; implausible values were discarded automatically, but a misread falling within plausible bounds would pass undetected.

06 Conclusion

Across three nights in the same room with the same occupants, air exchange rose from 0.10 h−1 sealed to 0.27 h−1 with a sliding door open and 0.33 h−1 with a fan exhausting through a narrow gap. Peak CO2 fell from 1496 to 979 ppm, and the fan night never crossed 1000 ppm at all — consistent with a fitted equilibrium of 934 ppm, below the threshold by construction rather than by luck.

Mechanical extraction through a small gap outperformed a large passive opening on both CO2 and particulates. If that holds up under repetition, it is a useful result for anyone who would rather not sleep with a door wide open.

The next step is not a more elaborate model but more nights: each configuration repeated at least three times, in alternating rather than ascending order, with occupancy recorded explicitly. Only then do these three curves become measurements rather than observations.

Data availability. 1280 readings collected 23–26 August 2026 at 3-minute intervals, stored in SQLite. Per-night summary statistics exportable as CSV via analyze.py --export-nights. Every reading retains the source photograph and a sidecar record of exactly what the transcription model returned.

Analysis. Python 3.13 with pandas and NumPy. Mass-balance parameters fitted by coarse-to-fine grid search over a shared S and per-night λ, minimising total sum of squared residuals across all three build-up phases.
